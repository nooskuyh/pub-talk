# Trapezius & Pectoralis Interaction

The shoulder girdle functions through an antagonistic force couple: anterior chest muscles and posterior back muscles continuously counter-balance each other to maintain scapulothoracic stability.

::card-grid{columns="2"}
::info-card{title="Pectoralis Complex (Anterior Pull)" icon="i-carbon:arrow-down-right" tone="coral"}
- **Pectoralis Minor**: Inserts into coracoid process; drives scapular protraction, depression, and anterior tilt.
- **Pectoralis Major**: Guides internal rotation and horizontal adduction of the humerus.
- **Biomechanical Vector**: Draws the shoulder girdle forward and inward toward the midline.
::

::info-card{title="Trapezius Group (Posterior Anchor)" icon="i-carbon:arrow-up-right" tone="teal"}
- **Middle Trapezius**: Primary retractor; anchors the medial border of the scapula to the spine.
- **Lower Trapezius**: Depresses and posteriorly tilts the scapula, opposing forward tipping.
- **Upper Trapezius**: Elevates the shoulder girdle and coordinates upward scapular rotation.
::
::

::note-box
**Dynamic Equilibrium:** When both groups maintain balanced resting tension, the scapula remains flush against the posterior thoracic wall, allowing unhindered glenohumeral range of motion.
::

# Rounded Shoulder Posture

Rounded shoulder posture (RSP) occurs when prolonged postural loading disrupts this muscular force couple, producing anterior translation, protracted scapulae, and internal humeral rotation.

::card-grid{columns="3" density="compact"}
::info-card{title="1. Pectoral Shortening" icon="i-carbon:warning-alt" tone="coral"}
Sustained forward posture induces adaptive shortening and hyperactivity in pectoralis minor and major, fixing the coracoid process forward.
::

::info-card{title="2. Trapezius Inhibition" icon="i-carbon:arrows-horizontal" tone="amber"}
Through reciprocal inhibition, hypertonic anterior muscles weaken and lengthen the middle and lower trapezius, causing scapular winging and instability.
::

::info-card{title="3. Postural Correction" icon="i-carbon:checkmark-outline" tone="teal"}
Correction requires simultaneously lengthening anterior chest muscles (myofascial release/stretches) and activating posterior stabilizers (Y/T raises).
::
::

::note-box
**Clinical Takeaway:** Rounded shoulders stem from a functional neurological imbalance—restoring posture requires addressing both anterior hyperactivity and posterior underactivity.
::
